Eight seconds to decide

It is 11:02 on a Friday morning in Japan. You are the Earthquake Early Warning system. Your sensors report shaking. If a strong earthquake is coming, a warning gives people a few seconds to get under a desk, stop a train, or pull a car over.

Shaking detected

Shaking detected, same moment

What you do: Select Start the clock. You have eight seconds to choose. No person can check the readings in that time.
8 seconds

What the real system did

On January 5, 2018, it sent the alarm. Phones and public loudspeakers sounded across the Tokyo region and part of Fukushima. The system had predicted one earthquake of magnitude 6.4.

What had happened

Two small earthquakes struck at almost the same moment: magnitude 4.4 off Ibaraki and magnitude 3.9 in Toyama. The system treated them as one large earthquake. No strong shaking came.

Was the system wrong to send the alarm? It made a false alarm. A false alarm cost millions of people a few anxious seconds. A missed warning before a real magnitude 6.4 earthquake costs lives. Someone chose, long before that morning, which error the system should lean toward. Tonight you make that kind of choice yourself.

What you do: A supply office asked a GenAI tool to summarize a shift note. Compare the summary with the note. Select every sentence in the summary that the note does not support. Then select Check my picks.

The shift note (the source)

Shipment 118 came in Tuesday. We received 40 of 48 boxes. The other 8 are on backorder and the vendor gave no date. Forklift 2 failed its inspection and cannot be used. Inventory count is Thursday at 8:00 in the morning.

The GenAI summary

Classifiers and extractors

Sentiment, entities, and key phrases pick a label from a fixed list or pull items out of the text. They fail with a wrong label or a missed item. You can see the error when you read the source. You check them by comparing a sample with a person's labels.

Generators (GenAI)

Summaries, drafted replies, and chatbots write new text. They fail by leaving something out or by stating what the source never said, and the text still reads smoothly. You check them the way you just did: against the source, line by line.

A real case: the city chatbot

In October 2023, New York City put a GenAI chatbot on its website to answer business owners' questions about city rules. In March 2024, reporters at The Markup tested it. It told them employers could keep part of their workers' tips, landlords could turn away tenants who pay with housing vouchers, and restaurants could refuse cash. All three answers were wrong under New York law. The answers read like official guidance, and nothing on the screen warned the reader.

Sort, spot, trigger

Organizations use AI outputs for three kinds of work. The same error rate is harmless in one and dangerous in another.

Before you judge an AI output, ask what the organization does with it.

Section 2 · Three uses for an output

What does the organization do with the output?

Sort

The output puts each item in a pile. A person then handles the pile.

Example: support tickets labeled "billing" go to the billing team.

Spot

The output shows a pattern across many items. Nobody acts on one item alone.

Example: complaints about one vendor doubled this quarter.

Trigger

The output starts an action automatically. No person stands between the output and the action.

Example: the earthquake alarm you just ran.

Section 4 · What you just did

The 100-item test

You do not need a vendor's accuracy claim. Anyone in an organization can run this test before the organization relies on a scoring system. You ran the last two steps in the threshold lab.

  1. Pull 100 real items from your own organization, with the messy wording people really use.
  2. Have someone who knows the work label each one first, without seeing the system's answer.
  3. Run the same 100 through the system.
  4. Count four numbers: problems it flagged, problems it missed, false alarms, and items it correctly left alone.
  5. Read the misses and sort them by type. Then check them by group. Does the system do worse on short messages, or on writers whose first language is not English?

Drift: language changes, so a system that passed this test last year can fail it this year with no warning. Run the test on a schedule.

Section 5 · What happened in the timed task

Automation bias

Automation bias is the habit of trusting an automated answer over your own judgment. It has two forms.

Missing what the system did not flag

The system says "OK," and the person stops checking. In the task: approving line 3 or line 7.

Acting on a wrong flag

The system flags something that is fine, and the person goes along. In the task: sending back line 5.

A real study: student pilots and an automated aid

In 1999, researchers put 80 students in a flight simulator. Half had an automated aid that announced events needing action. Half had only the gauges. The gauges were always correct. Sometimes the aid stayed silent when the gauges showed a problem. Students without the aid missed 3 percent of those problems.

Section 5 · A real case and six practices

A person in the process is not a review

In 2003, during the war in Iraq, United States Army Patriot air defense systems identified two friendly aircraft as enemy missiles and shot them down. Three aircrew died. A Defense Science Board review found that the system operated in a largely automatic way and that operators had been trained to trust its software. The board recommended more operator involvement and control, and changes to the software, the displays, and the training.

Operators were present both times. Being present did not give them the evidence, the training, or the practice to question the system.

Why careful people do this

The system is usually right. Its output looks certain. The reviewer has no time. Disagreeing costs more than agreeing. Telling people to "stay alert" does not change any of these. The process has to make checking the easy path.

Six practices that reduce automation bias

  1. Decide first, then look. The reviewer forms a view before the label appears.
  2. Keep the source beside the output. The label sits next to the full message. The summary sits next to the document.
  3. Get a second source for anything that matters. A flag starts a look at other evidence. A flag alone is never grounds for action.
  4. Audit what the system cleared. A random sample of the unflagged pile is the only way to find misses.
  5. Make disagreeing cheap. One click to overrule, no penalty, and a log of overrides that someone reads.
  6. Staff the review honestly. A reviewer with 15 seconds an item cannot check anything.

Section 6

High stakes: who decides

Some decisions are cheap to get wrong and easy to undo. Others change a person's life.

A decision is high-stakes when it affects someone's health, money, job, education, freedom, or safety, and when it is hard to undo. You design a review with a limited budget and run it for a month.

Section 6 · Three designs and five needs

How is the person involved?

DesignHow it worksExampleFits
Human in the loopThe system recommends. A person decides every case.A pharmacist approves each refill the system suggests.High stakes
Human on the loopThe system acts. A person watches and can step in or reverse it.A system locks an account after a risky login. An analyst watches, and the owner can unlock it with a call.Medium stakes, easy to undo
Human out of the loopThe system acts alone. A person audits a sample now and then.A junk mail filterLow stakes

Most failures come from running a high-stakes decision on a low-stakes design. High confidence never takes the person out of a high-stakes decision. A wrong answer with a high score is the most dangerous kind, because nobody questions it.

Five things a real review needs

TimeEnough to read the source, not only the label.
InformationThe original message or document, and what the system based its output on.
AuthorityThe power to overrule the system without asking permission.
A nameOne identified person or office answers for the decision. "The system flagged it" is not an answer.
A way backThe affected person is told, given a reason, and given a way to contest the decision.

Take away any one and the review is a formality.
